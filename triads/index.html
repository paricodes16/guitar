Theory

What is a triad?

A triad is a chord of three notes. You build one by choosing a note, called the root, and stacking two thirds on top of it. That gives three chord tones: the root (R), the third (3) and the fifth (5). Triads are the foundation of harmony, and most chords you know are triads with extra notes added.

Triads in the C major scale

The C major scale is C D E F G A B. To build a triad, start on a note, skip one, take the next, skip one, and take the next. Starting on C gives C, E, G. C to E is a major third (4 semitones) and E to G is a minor third (3 semitones), and that combination makes a major triad. Doing the same on every note of the scale gives seven triads.

NumeralChordNotesTypeFunction

Three types of triad. Major: major third then minor third (1 – 3 – 5). Minor: minor third then major third (1 – ♭3 – 5). Diminished: two minor thirds (1 – ♭3 – ♭5). In the numerals, capitals are major, lowercase are minor, and the small circle means diminished.

Inversions

Inverting a triad means changing which chord tone is the lowest note. The three notes stay the same, only their order changes. The chord keeps its name, and we add the bass note after a slash: C with E in the bass is written C/E for a C major chord.

Why learn inversions? They let you play the same chord in different places on the neck, and they let chords connect smoothly. For example, going from C major in root position (C E G) to F major in 2nd inversion (C F A), the C stays where it is, E steps up to F, and G steps up to A. Only two notes move, and each by one step.

Reading the fretboard

Each triad here sits on three neighbouring strings, called a string set. The bass note is on the lowest string of the set and the highest note is on the top string. The strings are drawn like tab, with the 6th string (low E) on top.

The colours show the inversions, and the white ring marks the root note of the chord wherever it appears. A shape repeats 12 frets higher, so you may see the same colour twice.

Shapes are movable. Slide a shape up or down by the same number of frets and you get a triad of the same type and inversion on a different root, as long as the shape has no open strings. Each string set has its own shapes, because the strings are tuned a fourth apart except for G to B, which is a major third.

How to practise

  1. Pick one string set and play the three inversions of C major one after another, saying each note name as you play it.
  2. Listen to the sound of each type. Major triads sound bright, minor triads sound darker, and the diminished triad sounds tense and unfinished.
  3. Keep the same string set and the same inversion, and move through the seven chords. Notice how the shape changes between major, minor and diminished.
  4. Repeat everything on a different string set.
